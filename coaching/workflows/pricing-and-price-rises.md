## 1. How to set a price: start from your costs

Deanna works out prices from the business's real costs, not by copying competitors:

1. **Fixed costs**: rent, insurance, software (booking system), utilities, loan or equipment repayments, accounting, marketing you pay for every month.
2. **Variable costs**: products and consumables used per service, card and booking fees, laundry.
3. **Wages**: staff wages **and the owner's own pay**. If you aren't paying yourself, the price is wrong.

Then check the result against local market rates. Deanna's rule from her courses: **don't undercut significantly. Compete on quality and professionalism, not price.**

> The coach never does this arithmetic in the chat. It sends the owner to the **pricing worksheet tool**, which shows each cost line, time per service, cost per service and margin. Missing inputs show as "Add data". The coach does not guess them.

## 2. When to raise prices

Raise prices when either is true:
- **Your costs go up**: rent, product prices, wages, insurance.
- **Demand is high**: you're booked out, have a waitlist, or turn people away.

Deanna's course rule: **review your prices every 6–12 months** even when nothing obvious has changed, so increases stay small and regular rather than large and rare.

## 3. How Deanna raises prices

- **No announcement.** Lash'd doesn't send a "prices are going up" message. The new price simply applies to **future bookings**.
- **Already-booked appointments keep the old price.** That's the fairness step: nobody is surprised at an appointment they booked at a known price.
- **Everyone moves together.** Regulars aren't kept on old pricing. All clients move to the new price for new bookings.

> **[PROPOSED]** The coach presents this as Deanna's approach. It also tells owners that some choose to give notice (for example a sign or a line on the booking page). Either is fine, as long as nobody is charged more than the price they booked at.

## 4. When a client questions the price

Deanna's answer, in substance: the price is what it takes to run the business properly, it reflects the **standard of professionalism and skill** clients receive, and the salon uses **top-of-the-market products**.

### Client script — [PROPOSED wording in Deanna's style]
> "Totally understand. Our prices reflect what it takes to run the salon properly: the training behind every service, the standard we work to, and the top-quality products we use on you. That's what keeps your results consistent every time you come in."

Rules for the conversation:
- Don't apologise for your price, and don't offer a discount just to end the conversation. Planned specials are different (see §6).
- Explain once, calmly, then leave it. The client decides.

## 5. The biggest beginner mistakes

1. **Charging too little.** Underpricing undervalues your training, your time and the standard you're held to, and it isn't sustainable.
2. **Not charging friends and family, out of guilt.** A free or heavily discounted service still costs you products, time and a booking slot a paying client could have had.
3. **Discounting everyone.** If everyone gets a discount, it isn't a discount. It's your real price, and it's too low.

## 6. Specials and new-client offers (Deanna, 16 Nov 2026)

> There needs to be a special of some sort sometimes.

Targeted specials are a legitimate tool, especially for owners who want **new clients**. Deanna has run a **discounted Korean lash lift for new clients only, promoted through a Meta ad**. The difference from the mistakes above:

| Good special | Problem discounting |
|---|---|
| Has a purpose (e.g. win new clients, fill a quiet period, launch a service) | Given out of guilt, habit or fear |
| Limited to a group or time (new clients only, a set date) | Everyone, all the time |
| Costed first, so you know what each one costs you | Never costed |
| Designed to lead to full-price rebooking | No plan for what happens next |
| Measured: how many booked, how many came back at full price | Not tracked |

**[PROPOSED] How the coach helps with a special:**
1. Run the pricing worksheet first, so the owner knows the special still covers product cost (or knows exactly what each one costs as an acquisition cost).
2. Make it new-client only (or time-limited) and say so clearly.
3. Plan the follow-up: rebook in the chair at full price, plus the loyalty program.
4. Track new clients from the offer, cost per new client, and **how many came back at full price**. That last number decides whether the special worked.

## How the coach uses this

**Typical triggers:** "how much should I charge", "am I too cheap", "should I put my prices up", "how do I tell clients", "a client said I'm too expensive", "my friends want free lashes".

**Check first** (profile/project; ask only what's missing):
1. Current service menu and prices.
2. Time per service, and product/consumable cost per service (rough is fine; the worksheet handles it).
3. Monthly fixed costs and wages, including whether the owner pays themselves.
4. Capacity: how full the diary is, and whether there's a waitlist.
5. When prices last changed.

**Then:**
- Run the **pricing worksheet** with them. Never invent the numbers.
- If costs have risen or demand is high, recommend a rise and explain Deanna's no-announcement method.
- Give the client script for price questions.
- If friends, family or blanket discounts are the issue, address the guilt directly.
- **Next action:** update prices in the booking system for new bookings. **Measure:** average spend per visit and bookings per week over the next 4–8 weeks, compared with before.

**Limits:** prices shown to consumers must comply with local consumer law (for example, displaying the full price including any mandatory fees). GST treatment of prices goes to an accountant.
